## Key Takeaways

1. **Evidence for autistic children is mixed but net-positive, and technology-based
   approaches specifically rank well.** A 2026 network meta-analysis found
   **Technology-Aided Instruction and Intervention (TAII) ranked first**
   among compared emotion-regulation intervention types for autistic
   children, ahead of drawing therapy and floortime, with TAII and CBT
   named as most effective overall. This is a direct, favorable data point
   for building an app-based intervention rather than a caution against it.
   However, effectiveness is described as **context-dependent**, supporting
   a personalized/stepped-care approach rather than one-size-fits-all
   content.

2. **Outcomes across the autism intervention literature overall are mixed**:
   one systematic review breakdown found roughly 48% of studies reporting
   improvements, 40% mixed results, 12% no significant effect. This is a
   caution against overclaiming efficacy in the app's own marketing —
   consistent with Principle 3 (skill-building, not therapy; no treatment
   claims) — and a reason to keep expectations, and any future claims,
   modest and specific rather than broad ("helps kids build a vocabulary
   for feelings," not "reduces meltdowns").

3. **Group-based interventions involving parents/teachers show promise for
   socio-emotional competence** — all six group-based interventions in one
   review were effective on that outcome, though the specific skills
   targeted varied. This supports building in a real parent-side role
   (PLAN.md §5) rather than treating the parent surface as an afterthought
   — parent involvement isn't just nice-to-have, it shows up as a factor in
   effectiveness.

4. **A key methodological caution**: several autism intervention studies
   lack comparator/control groups, making it hard to attribute improvement
   to the intervention itself vs. other factors (maturation, concurrent
   therapy, etc.). This matters for how the app *itself* should eventually
   be evaluated if efficacy claims are ever made — don't rely on
   pre/post-only data internally either.

5. **For ADHD, the evidence base for digital/app interventions specifically
   is thin.** The JMIR narrative review found only **5 RCTs, 1
   quasi-experimental study, 4 open-label uncontrolled studies, and 2
   ongoing studies without results** on non-pharmacological emotion
   regulation *digital* interventions for ADHD — explicitly described as
   "very little direct evidence of what works" in this specific niche.
   General (non-digital) evidence-based approaches for ADHD emotion
   regulation include CBT, DBT, ACT, mindfulness-based interventions, and
   behavioral parent training (the latter has substantial evidence for
   reducing disruptive behavior and ADHD symptoms specifically).

6. **This means the ADHD track is more of a research bet than the autism
   track.** The app can draw on well-evidenced *non-digital* modalities
   (mindfulness, behavioral strategies) when designing ADHD-relevant
   content, but should not claim to be evidence-based *as an app* for ADHD
   emotion regulation specifically — that evidence largely doesn't exist
   yet in the literature.

## What This Changes In The Plan

- **Technology-based intervention is evidence-supported for the autism use
  case specifically** — reinforces going ahead with an app-based approach
  rather than treating it as an unproven bet, at least for that population.
- **Marketing/positioning (Principle 3, Open Decision #9) should avoid
  outcome claims** (e.g., "reduces meltdowns," "improves emotional
  regulation" as a headline claim) given the mixed overall evidence and
  thin ADHD-specific digital evidence — "skill-building," "practice," and
  "vocabulary for feelings" language is both more honest and lower legal/
  reputational risk than efficacy claims.
- **Parent-side involvement (PLAN.md §5) is reinforced as a real feature,
  not a nice-to-have** — group/parent-involved interventions show up
  favorably in the evidence, so the parent surface should include active
  guidance/coaching content, not just a passive dashboard.
- **For ADHD-specific content, ground it in the well-evidenced non-digital
  modalities** (mindfulness, CBT-style reframing made age-appropriate,
  behavioral strategies) rather than assuming any digital-specific
  approach is validated — this is a content-design note for Phase 2, and a
  reason *not* to make ADHD-specific efficacy claims.
- **Supports "personalized, stepped-care" framing** — i.e., not all
  children need the same path through content — which may be relevant to
  Open Decision #2 (age range / diagnosis-specific tracks): the evidence
  leans toward *some* differentiation being valuable, though it doesn't
  specify how.
